What the Numbers Mean for Your Paycheck

At $100,000 gross income, a W-2 employee takes home about $79,180 while a 1099 contractor takes home about $74,255. The W-2 scenario keeps roughly $4,925 more. The gap comes mainly from self-employment tax — as a 1099 contractor, you pay both the employer and employee portions of Social Security and Medicare.

How 1099 and W-2 Are Taxed Differently

W-2 employees pay 7.65% FICA — 6.2% Social Security plus 1.45% Medicare. The employer pays the other 7.65%. You never see that half on your paystub, but it's being covered.

1099 contractors pay 15.3% self-employment tax — both halves combined. But two adjustments soften the blow: first, SE tax is calculated on 92.35% of your earnings, not the full amount (IRC §1402(a)(12)). Second, you deduct 50% of SE tax from your income before calculating federal income tax (IRC §164(f)).

The net effect: at $100,000 gross, a 1099 contractor pays about $14,130 in SE tax versus $7,650 in W-2 FICA. Even after the 50% deduction reduces your income tax, you still come out behind by roughly $4,925.

What happens at higher income?

At $200,000 gross, the W-2 advantage grows to $10,507. But at $300,000, it shrinks slightly to $8,814. That surprises most people. Here's why: Social Security tax is capped at $184,500 in wages. Once you pass that threshold, neither classification pays the 6.2% or 12.4% SS portion on additional income. Meanwhile, the 50% SE deduction keeps reducing the 1099 contractor's taxable income at higher brackets — so the gap narrows even though the 1099 contractor still pays more Medicare (2.9% vs 1.45% on every dollar).

How the Same Income Gets Taxed Differently

W-2 path: gross → standard deduction → taxable income → federal brackets → income tax + employee FICA (6.2% SS up to $184,500, 1.45% Medicare) → take-home.

1099 path: gross → 92.35% SE base → self-employment tax (12.4% SS up to $184,500, 2.9% Medicare) → 50% SE deduction → adjusted gross → standard deduction → taxable income → federal brackets → income tax + SE tax → take-home.

The 1099 side assumes all gross receipts are net earnings (Schedule C with no business expenses). Self-employment tax is computed per Schedule SE. This is a federal-only estimate for a single filer using the 2026 standard deduction.

Limitations

  • No state or local taxes. State income tax can add 5–13% on top of federal in high-tax states like California or New York. Ignoring it makes the 1099 gap look smaller than it really is.
  • No QBI deduction. Section 199A allows up to a 20% deduction on qualified business income, which can significantly reduce 1099 federal income tax. Not modeling it means the 1099 side overstates tax burden.
  • Single filer only. Married filing jointly has different brackets and a $32,200 standard deduction. Results would differ substantially.
  • Additional Medicare Tax: 0.9% on wages above $200,000 (single) / $250,000 (MFJ) — included in W-2 FICA and 1099 SE calculations.
  • No business expense deductions. The 1099 side assumes net earnings equal gross income. Real contractors may deduct eligible business expenses, which can reduce both income tax and self-employment tax.
  • No employer benefit valuation. W-2 compensation may include health insurance, retirement match, paid time off, or other benefits not shown in take-home pay.
  • Tax year 2026 only. Brackets and thresholds change annually. Values may not be current.

FAQ

Why does 1099 show lower take-home at the same gross?
Because 1099 contractors pay self-employment tax at 15.3% — both the employer and employee halves of FICA. W-2 employees only pay 7.65%; the employer covers the rest. That difference alone accounts for most of the take-home gap.

What is the 92.35% self-employment tax adjustment?
The IRS doesn't make you pay SE tax on 100% of your earnings. Under IRC §1402(a)(12), only 92.35% of your net self-employment earnings are subject to SE tax. This roughly accounts for the employer-half deduction that W-2 employees don't pay.

Does this include the QBI deduction?
No. The Qualified Business Income deduction (Section 199A) can reduce 1099 income tax by up to 20% of qualified business income. This tool does not model it, so the 1099 side may overstate your actual tax burden.

Does this include state taxes?
No. This is federal-only. If you live in a state with income tax (California, New York, etc.), your real take-home will be lower for both classifications — and the 1099 gap may grow wider.

Is a $X/hour 1099 rate equal to the same $X/hour W-2 rate?
No. A $50/hour 1099 contract and a $50/hour W-2 wage are not equivalent after taxes. The 1099 rate needs to be higher to compensate for the self-employment tax burden. Annualize both rates and run them through this calculator to see the real difference.
